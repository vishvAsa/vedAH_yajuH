01: The Pravargya as the head of the Sacrifice

This anuvāka contains the mythological story of the origin and purpose, the purākalpa, of the Pravargya ceremony in the form of the story of Makha. See Introduction section 3.4 and Hillebrandt, Vedische Mythologie:414-54; Keith, Religion:112; Rönnow, “Zur Erklärung des Pravargya etc.":113ff, esp. 127; Eggeling, SBE 44, p. xlvi-l; van Buitenen, Pravargya:16ff; Gonda, Aspects: 167ff; Kashikar, “Mahāvīra"; and Heesterman, “The Case of the Severed Head” in: Essays in Indian Ritual, Kingship and Society, Chicago 1985. In the introduction of the TĀ edition C (p. 27-28), a translation of this anuvāka is quoted from Muir, Sanskrit Texts IV, p. 112.

If one places the Pravargya vessel on the fire, then one puts back the head of the sacrifice. Offering that sacrifice united with its head, one gains blessings, one conquers the celestial world.

Therefore (because they put the head of the sacrifice on the body), this Pravargya is strong (in its mantras and sacrificial acts) for the Aśvins, as it were.

11: The different names of the Pravargya Ritual

In this anuvāka we find different names of the Pravargya, in order to praise it according to one commentator (S), for the sake of the performance of expiations (prāyaścittārtha) according to the other (B). The text of this anuvāka gives no indication that the different names, given to the Pravargya in the successive stages of the performance, were closely connected with expiation rites like e.g. those of SB 12, 6, 1 (discussed by J. 12: Metamorphoses of the Pravargya

  160. púnyartim tásmai kāmayate; cf. TB 3, 2, 3,7 pradātré púnyam asaste. The accentuation of púṇyārtim (on the first member of the compound) is in accordance with the regular accentuation in the older language of a compound with an action noun on -ti as the second member (Wackernagel-Debrunner, Altindische Grammatik*,* II (Göttingen 1957), 1:214 (§90a)). The usual translations of ārti are “painful occurrence, pain, injury, mischief; sickness” (e.g. Monier-Williams, Sanskrit-English Dictionary, P. 149; delete “for 2.ārti see s.v.”). S and Bh interpret árti in this way (explaining púnyārtim as bhagyahānim (S) and puṇyanāśam (Bh)), and this creates two problems. First, the context forces them to attributé a special connotation to yathānāmám*.* S: “(merely) with his own given name” (tadīyam nārnānatikramya nāmakaraṇakāle sampāditenaiva nāmnā); Bh: “with a name not made for the (special) purpose” (anarthakṛtena nāmnā). More serious is the second problem: the person “who knows thus” (yá eyám véda) obtains something undesirable (“mischief or damage to his merit”), whereas the one “who knows thus” is elsewhere always rewarded with something desirable. Bh sensed the problem, and gave an alternative interpretation of árti as vyasana. He probably had in mind “arduous devotion to”, one of the meanings of the latter word; but árti itself is nowhere else attested in that meaning. The solution proposed here is the following. The Vedic verb ār (ā + r) means “to reach, obtain”, and is usually pregnant with a negative connotation (“to reach mischief”), but not always. Cf. for the latter case Grassmann, Wörterbuch zum Rig- Veda:99, under ar+a, and Sūryakānta, A Practical Vedic Dictionary: 145, under ār. The derivative action noun árti may have been used here in the neutral sense of “obtainment.” In the dictionaries it has been recorded only in the negative sense, which it has, it is true, in almost all cases: “obtainment of mischief: painful occurrence etc. " If púnya in púnyartim is adjectival, the translation is: “good, pure or meritorious obtainment.” ↩︎
